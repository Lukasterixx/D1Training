# Bypass the arm to amplify its force

Published work already shows that a legged body can raise the force its arm applies. None of it establishes what Lukas's simulations point to, and that gap could support a credible paper if hardware backs it. Measured body-on versus body-off gains in the literature are modest: **about 1.3× on an ANYmal with a Kinova arm (RoLoMa) and 1.4–1.6× on a Unitree G1 humanoid (Thor)**. Portela et al.'s headline 90 N pull from a Z1 arm rated at 36 N compares a peak pull with a payload rating, not with an arm-only pull. Bracing, the classic parallel load path, dates to 1984–85, and its closest legged versions are two September 2026 preprints: a humanoid bracing hand ("Brace Yourself") and a brace frame mounted on a Go2's body (CTCS). Read in full, none of these papers identifies the arm's joint torque as the binding limit, explains the posture cap as a load path in series with the arm, compares arm-only, body-posture and arm-bypassing strategies on one task, reports lever or door torque in N·m, or uses an arm anywhere near as weak as the D1. Lukas's results sit in that gap. In simulation the arm alone pushes the combiner lever to about 1.15 N·m and the grip slips from 0.5 N·m, while a static model of crouching with the arm rigid gives a median of 0.94 and a best of 1.5 N·m. That is about 1.3×, the ratio the literature measures, but caused by the shoulder and elbow rather than by balance. **Every one of those numbers comes from simulation or a static model using Unitree's published torque limits; nothing has been measured on the robot, and the published limits themselves are unverified.** A credible paper would claim the mechanism and the controlled comparison, not "the body adds force". It would need a lever the arm alone cannot turn, measured lever torque and arm load on hardware, and 10–20 trials per condition. The calendar suits a staged route: a CoRL 2026 workshop paper by about 9 October 2026, an RSS 2027 Stage 1 abstract by 4 December 2026 built from Thesis B simulation, and Thesis C hardware for RSS Stage 2 (16 April 2027) or for IROS or CASE (1 March 2027).

## Published body gains cluster at 1.3–1.6×, where Lukas's static model also lands

Lukas's three findings frame the question. With the dog lying and the arm pushing the lever along its arc, the simulated arm holds the lever at 45° against a spring needing **up to 1.15 N·m with the box straight ahead and 0.9 N·m at a −43° bearing**. PhysX's projected torques put the base-yaw joint at 100% of its published 3.3 N·m from 0.8 N·m onward ([F-071](../results/findings.md#f-071)). Gripping the lever and pulling opens the door against springs up to 0.4 N·m, but **from 0.5 N·m the simulated grip loses the lever** while the arm still has torque to spare: the static ceiling for that grasp was 0.78 N·m ([F-073](../results/findings.md#f-073)), and only two grasps hold at all ([F-075](../results/findings.md#f-075)). A static model drops the standing dog 8.3 cm onto the lever with the arm held still, over 439 placements. It gives a **median of 0.94 N·m, a 90th percentile of 1.17 and a best of 1.51**. Shoulder pitch (Joint2) binds at 70% of placements and the elbow (Joint3) at the rest, while the dog's weight of about 180 N is ample, since the lever needs only about 15 N per N·m ([F-077](../results/findings.md#f-077)). The median crouch is therefore weaker than the lying push, and only the best stance beats it, by about 1.3×. All of these are simulation and static-model numbers. They rest on the D1-550's published limits of **3.3 N·m on the first two joints and 1.7 N·m on the last four** ([motor_model.py](../motor_model.py)), which Unitree's current D1-T page does not list at all ([Unitree D1-T](https://www.unitree.com/mobile/D1-T/)). The real handle's torque, the real servos under load and the real gripper's squeeze are all unmeasured. The arm's mount and base were also found later to sit 28.2 mm higher and 19 mm further back than the model these results used, so F-071 and F-077 need re-running before they go into a paper ([F-125](../results/findings.md#f-125), [F-126](../results/findings.md#f-126)).

The cap follows from standard statics. The arm's joint torques are τ_arm = g_arm(q) − J_eᵀ w_e, and when the feet are the only other contacts the arm's rows contain no leg terms. The body can therefore only re-orient the arm relative to the lever force, change the arm's gravity load, or add a contact. In force-polytope terms, a force that passes through the arm and then the legs is bounded by the **intersection** of the arm's polytope with the body's feasible wrench set, so it can never exceed what the arm alone allows. Force paths in parallel instead combine by **Minkowski sum**. Both operations are available off the shelf for exact torque polytopes that include gravity ([Skuric et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.05226); [pycapacity](https://auctus-team.gitlabpages.inria.fr/people/antunskuric/pycapacity/pycapacity.robot.html)), and legged feasibility sets are already built per limb and combined the same way ([Orsolino et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.02731)). Along a single required direction such as the lever tangent, the maximum force is a closed-form minimum over the joints, and that minimum names the bottleneck joint, which is what F-071 and F-077 report. No paper has published this decomposition as the explanation for legged force gains; it is a framing assembled from published tools.

The legged literature's measured gains fit this framing without stating it. RoLoMa is the cleanest controlled comparison. On an ANYmal B with a Kinova arm, its planner predicted a worst-case resistible force of 58 N with the base fixed at its default and only the arm moving, 57 N when the whole body re-postured to minimise effort, and 76 N when it re-postured to maximise that force. A force gauge measured **about 45 N against 60 N, "approximately 33 % better"**, with measurements 21–22% below prediction ([RoLoMa](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.01446v2)). Re-posturing for effort gained nothing; only re-posturing for force did. The authors explicitly decline to identify which constraint sets the bound, whether friction or torque, and on which foot or motor. Thor adds a load-dependent centre-of-mass lean to a G1 humanoid, derived from moment balance at the feet. Over 20 trials per condition, the lean raised dual-hand pulling from **104.6 to 167.7 N backward and from 103.6 to 145.5 N forward**, and arm joint torque is never discussed ([Thor v4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26280v4)). Portela et al. (ICRA 2024) recorded "the highest pulling force we can achieve", **90 N, against the Z1's 36 N rated payload**. Some summaries attach a dynamometer to that figure. The full text uses the dynamometer only in a separate 0–70 N downward-tracking test and gives no instrument, direction or trial count for the 90 N pull. It runs no arm-only pull, and arm torque enters only as a small reward penalty above 80% of the limit ([Portela et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.01402v2)). FORTE, a September 2026 preprint, optimises base pose for a residual force polytope "computed solely from the arm actuator torque limits", so its gains are series-path gains by construction ([FORTE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21497v1)). An earlier search recorded it as a *Robotics and Autonomous Systems* paper, but the full-text check found no venue on its arXiv record. FALCON writes the arm-torque bound as a per-axis force limit, f_max = min_j (τ_lim − τ_g)/|J|, but uses it to shape a training curriculum rather than to look for paths around it ([FALCON](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06776)). Sumo uprighted a 15 kg tire with a Spot arm rated to lift 11 kg, but measured no forces, and the tire pivots on the ground rather than hanging from the arm ([Sumo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.08508v3)).

The arms in these studies differ by about ten times in joint torque: the Z1 is rated at 33 N·m per joint ([ROS Components](https://www.roscomponents.com/product/z1-arm/)) against the D1's published 3.3 N·m. Yet posture-only gains land at 1.3–1.6× in every case, and the papers attribute them to balance, support polygon or friction. The D1 case differs in a way that matters. The dog weighs about eight times what the best crouch needs (180 N against about 23 N), so balance cannot be binding, and the static model says the arm is. That suggests two regimes the literature has not separated: a **balance-bound** regime, where posture helps directly, and an **arm-bound** regime, where posture helps only by reshaping the arm's Jacobian. The D1 is weak enough to sit clearly in the second. One possible reason arm torque never shows up in Thor is that its rope pull is horizontal at hand height, which may put straight arms in a near-singular "tie" posture that carries load with little joint torque. Thor reports no arm posture or torques, so this is an inference.

## Parallel force paths are forty years old for fixed arms and barely started on legged ones

Bracing is the canonical way around a weak joint, and its lineage is long. Book et al. proposed in 1984 that a light arm be "rigidized by bracing against either the workpiece or an auxiliary static structure", leaving only the distal joints to work ([Book et al.](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4615-9882-4_20)). West and Asada formalised the kinematics of braced manipulators in 1985 ([cited in Johnston et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.05032)). Their "mechanical advantage of braced manipulators" is the classical name for what Lukas calls force paths that bypass the arm's motors, though those papers were not read in full. The modern revival on fixed-base arms quantifies the relief. Fang et al. (ICRA 2019) landed a Centauro arm's elbow on a support plane, and the joint-torque norm fell by **60.8–87.1%** against holding the same posture without contact. That figure appears to come from simulation; hardware is shown only as a plot ([Fang et al.](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/157251188/ICRA2019_Cheng_Fang.pdf)). Natarajan et al. (ICRA 2023) report that bracing contacts reduce the torque a Kinova Gen3 needs ([Natarajan et al.](http://biorobotics.ri.cmu.edu/papers/paperUploads/85259-2703.pdf); not read in full). Šifrer and Petrič's 2026 *Scientific Reports* paper was taken for paywalled in earlier searches, but it is CC BY 4.0 open access and its full manuscript is downloadable. It uses the words Lukas would: a passive support under a 1.4 m tool "can in principle provide an additional load path through the environment" ([Šifrer & Petrič](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-60582-y_reference.pdf)). The robot is a Franka FR3 with a flange F/T sensor regulating 8 N, and the paper reports no numeric torque reduction and no trial counts, calling itself "a mechanism-level demonstration". Human ergonomics agrees: one-hand force rises sharply when the body can press on a support, "due to added chain of force vectors through the body" ([summary of Kroemer 1974](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169814126000892)).

"Brace Yourself" (QUT, arXiv 21 September 2026, submitted to RA-L) brings bracing to a Unitree G1: one hand braces on a planar surface while the other pushes. On hardware the robot sustained **60 N at the centre and upper positions and 40 N at the lower ones** ([Brace Yourself](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25486v1)). The abstract compares this with "a maximum sustained force of 13.5 N without environmental bracing" ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25486)). The full text shows that this 13.5 N baseline is a different system, the robot's onboard locomotion policy with IK and a PI force loop in an upright stance, so **controller, stance and bracing all change at once**. The clean bracing-only ablation exists only in simulation, where bracing raised episode success from 0.020 to 0.666 over 30 seeds. The authors blame the unbraced cap on balance: the baseline "cannot maintain the forward lean required to balance large reaction forces". They choose the support pose for ZMP and friction rather than to unload the working arm, and penalise arm torque only to manage heating. The working arm still carries the full task force in series. The 4–5× figure that circulates from the abstract should therefore not be cited as a bracing effect. The paper's simulated no-bracing case is the nearest published analogue to Lukas's crouch: a forward lean without a second contact "routes body load through the working hand". It is framed, though, as an unwanted minimum force on a humanoid, not as a torque cap.

CTCS, "Contact as a Decision Variable" (George Mason, arXiv 24 September 2026), is the paper a reviewer will raise first. Earlier searches listed it twice under its two names, but it is one paper, and it uses **a Go2 with an AgileX NERO 7-DoF arm and a rigid support frame on the side of the body** ([CTCS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.30140v1)). It selects one of nine environmental surfaces and a whole-body configuration, scored on residual wrench, reach and mobility. Across 392 Gazebo conditions (5–70 N in 14 directions), it certified **91.8% of tasks, against 68.4% (sticking) and 65.3% (walking) with ground-only support**. Torque limits on all 19 joints are hard constraints, but their values are not given and the paper never says which constraint binds. The arm's sampled configurations deliberately stay away from singularities. The hardware evidence is five trials each of one sticking task and one walking task, with a 25 N peak. The no-support case appears only as the qualitative remark that "the robot will roll over" ([CTCS supplementary](https://supplementary-material1.pages.dev/supplementary.pdf)). The crucial point for positioning is that **CTCS's brace is in parallel with the legs, not with the arm**. It relieves rollover and ground limits, the balance-bound regime, while the end-effector wrench still runs through all seven NERO joints. The NERO is a 3 kg-payload arm costing roughly €3,600 ([OpenELAB](https://openelab.io/products/agilex-nero-embodied-ai-arm)), far stronger than the D1.

Other routes around the arm have been demonstrated without handle-level numbers. Pedipulate pressed a door handle down with an ANYmal foot and pushed through a door with a spring-damper hinge, reporting no handle torque ([Pedipulate](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10837)). Sleiman et al. used feet to hold spring-loaded doors open and to help with a dishwasher whose joint needed up to 125 N ([Sleiman et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.09179)). Jeon et al.'s 27 kg quadruped with no arm pushes a 19.2 kg drum with its body ([Jeon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16820)). FWBC-VLA's base compensation raised success at pushing a door against a closer, about 50 N at the handle, from 0% to 52%, and its authors attribute the gain to stability rather than added capacity ([FWBC-VLA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03889v2)). For tools, Holladay et al.'s "forceful kinematic chain" chooses grasps, spanners and fixtures on fixed arms so that no joint-torque or friction limit is exceeded ([Holladay et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00319)). ETH's ANYmal door policy uses a hook trained against 0–3 N·m of handle resistance and succeeded in 38 of 40 real traversals ([Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04882v1)). The hook solves slip, not torque. Slip is also where RoLoMa's bucket lift failed, at 5.1 kg, when "the handle slipped off the gripper" ([RoLoMa](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.01446v2)), the same failure as F-073.

Two further routes remain largely unexplored. A straight arm in a near-singular "strut" posture can carry load along its links with almost no joint torque ([Lynch & Park](https://modernrobotics.northwestern.edu/nu-gm-book-resource/velocity-kinematics-and-statics/)), and mechanism designers use this deliberately as singularity locking ([Plooij et al.](https://filelist.tudelft.nl/me/Organisatie/Afdelingen/BmechE/DBL/PlooijLocking.pdf)). No paper found plans such postures for a legged manipulator, and CTCS excludes them by design. Body–ground contact has one older precedent: Wolfslag et al. (IROS 2020) put prongs on a quadruped's body to create body–ground contact that augments manipulation. It was seen only as a search summary and must be read before any novelty claim about body contact ([Edinburgh Research Explorer](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/optimisation-of-body-ground-contact-for-augmenting-whole-body-loc)).

## The open contribution is where the force flows, measured in newton-metres

Much of this ground is already covered. Published work shows the body raising arm force (Portela, Thor, RoLoMa, Sumo) and force-capability metrics that account for torque limits (RoLoMa, FORTE, FALCON). It covers bracing (Book, Fang, Brace Yourself, CTCS), legs and body used as manipulators (Pedipulate, Sleiman, Jeon), tool choice under torque limits (Holladay) and hook-based door opening (ETH). Read in full, though, the closest papers leave five things open. First, no paper compares **arm-only, body-posture and arm-bypassing strategies on one task on a quadruped**: RoLoMa and Thor compare posture on and off, and CTCS compares support choices by feasibility. Second, no paper explains the posture cap as a **series load path**. Šifrer and Petrič and Fang anticipate the wording on fixed arms, while the legged papers blame balance or leave the binding constraint unidentified. Third, no paper uses a **weak, position-commanded servo arm** whose joint torque binds first. Fourth, no paper reports the **lever or door torque achieved, in N·m**. ETH randomises handle resistance only in simulation, FWBC-VLA gives about 50 N at a handle, and RoLoMa's often-quoted lever figures (50–69 rising to 68–75 N) are planner-computed robustness margins, not measured lever forces. Fifth, no paper uses **near-singular arm postures** as a deliberate force path. A paper that delivered the first four with hardware would be new relative to everything found; the fifth is a riskier extension.

The D1 makes the arm-bound regime unambiguous, and it makes the hardware fragile. It is the weakest arm in this literature, with a 500 g payload ([Unitree D1-T](https://www.unitree.com/mobile/D1-T/)) and published joint torques about a tenth of the Z1's. Only one paper uses it for manipulation tasks: a 2026 tactile preprint that tightens a valve without reporting torque and states that the D1 "only accepts joint-position commands at 10 Hz" ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27224v3)). MERL's Go2 + D1 paper added a BOTA wrist F/T sensor for admittance control and gives the D1's send/receive limit as 40 Hz rather than 10 ([Schperberg et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.02443)). A 2025 Caltech student report says Unitree had stopped selling the D1 by March 2025, and that the unit's servos ran "burning hot" before it failed ([Zeng et al.](https://eloisezeng.people.caltech.edu/documents/32338/2025_SURF_Final_Report.pdf)). A low-rate, position-only arm favours strategies that need no fast force control, such as bracing, body contact or a fixed tool, which suits this direction. But the paper's premise rests on torque limits that no current Unitree page publishes, and a search-engine claim of 23.5 N·m could not be found on any page ([STEMfinity](https://stemfinity.com/products/d1-servo-arm)). A bench measurement of the D1's stall torques is therefore the first experiment, not a detail.

The strongest objection is that the result is obvious. τ = Jᵀf is textbook, West and Asada named braced mechanical advantage forty years ago, and a reviewer can call a 1.3× posture cap expected. The answer is that the legged literature has not stated it, attributes its own posture gains to balance, and declines to identify binding constraints, and that the measured ratios across platforms have no published explanation. The paper should therefore present itself as a quantified revival of bracing on a legged robot, cite Book and West–Asada prominently, and claim the controlled comparison and the hardware measurement rather than the principle. The second risk is speed. Five closely related preprints appeared in a single month: FWBC-VLA (3 September), the force-aware wheeled-legged RL paper ([arXiv 2609.13779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.13779v1), 12 September), FORTE (18 September), Brace Yourself (21 September) and CTCS (24 September). The CTCS group is a short step from Lukas's claim: they would only need to put the brace on the arm side and report the binding constraint. The search should be re-run before every submission, and Wolfslag et al. (2020), Natarajan et al. (2023) and UniFP ([Zhi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20829v1)) should be read in full.

## A credible paper needs a lever the arm cannot turn and a sensor on the lever

The combiner box can carry an amplification paper only if its real lever is stiff. The real handle's torque at 45° is still an open Week 1 item. The demonstrator's springs of 0.3–0.4 N·m sit below what the simulated arm turns unaided ([F-071](../results/findings.md#f-071)), so if the real lever is similar, the box opens without any amplification. The paper needs a lever of adjustable stiffness, and ideally a spring-loaded door, swept from below the arm-only ceiling (about 1–1.5 N·m in simulation) to well above it, so that each strategy gets a curve of success against required torque. ETH's 0–3 N·m handle range is a defensible span for real door handles ([Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04882v1)). The decisive measurement is torque at the lever's spindle, from an inline torque sensor or load cell. Alongside it, the arm's joint load at the peak shows whether a bypass really unloads the shoulder and elbow. That load could come from servo current or tracking error, if the D1 exposes them, or from a wrist F/T sensor as in MERL's setup.

The conditions should run on the same rig. They are the arm alone with the body fixed, the crouch with the arm rigid (the series case), and at least one, preferably two, parallel paths. Candidate paths are the Go2's body or a bumper mounted on it pressing the lever, a front foot on the lever in the style of Pedipulate, the forearm braced on the box, or a lever tool. The tool needs care. A rod clamped in the gripper and pushing with its tip lowers the force needed, but the wrist must then resist the rod's moment, and the wrist servos are the weakest joints at 1.7 N·m. A tool that couples rigidly to the handle, so that the lever's own spindle reacts the extra moment like a cheater bar, is a genuine parallel path. The headroom is large. At F-077's 6.5 cm contact radius, 180 N of body weight corresponds to about 12 N·m if none of it passes through the arm. That is roughly eight times the best series value, and an arithmetic ceiling before leg torque, friction and tipping are counted.

The stronger papers set the bar for statistics and modelling. Thor ran 20 hardware trials per condition and reported mean ± SE ([Thor v4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26280v4)), ETH ran 20 per door side, and UniFP ran 50 per task ([UniFP](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20829v1)), so 10–20 trials per condition with confidence intervals is the minimum. RoLoMa's predicted-versus-measured comparison is the model to copy: for each stance and strategy, publish the static polytope prediction and then the measured value. Each case should also name its binding constraint (arm joint, grip friction, foot friction or tipping), which RoLoMa and CTCS did not do. The pull side should be reported honestly as grip-limited first (F-073), with a hook or tool as the fix. CoRL requires a Limitations section and encourages real-robot evidence ([CoRL CFP](https://www.corl.org/contributions/call-for-papers)). A learned whole-body policy conditioned on force capability would raise the paper's reach toward CoRL, but the core claim does not need one. The thesis plan's pre-attached lever tool and its P3/P4 tool comparisons are the natural host ([Thesis B plan](../docs/thesis_b_plan.md)), provided the tool is designed as a spindle-coupled parallel path rather than a pusher held in the gripper. The order of work follows from this. Bench-measure the D1's joint torques, the real handle and the gripper's squeeze; re-run F-071 and F-077 on the F-126 geometry; simulate the parallel paths; and only then build the rig and measure.

## Conclusion

The ~1.3× crouch gain is not a disappointing result; it is the number that turns a demonstration into a mechanism paper. Published posture-only gains converge on 1.3–1.6× across robots whose arms differ ten times in strength, and no paper says why. The D1 is weak enough that body weight exceeds what the lever needs by about eight times. That makes it the cleanest platform for showing that the arm, not balance, sets the ceiling, and that only force paths in parallel with the arm lift it. The "obvious in principle" objection is answered by being first to measure the effect on a legged robot and to name the binding constraint, not by claiming the principle.

The thesis demonstrator and the paper need different things. The demonstrator needs the real box opened at whatever its lever takes, which may be within the arm alone. The paper needs a lever the arm cannot turn, a torque sensor on its spindle, and a D1 whose limits have been measured rather than read from a README. Measuring the real handle and the D1's stall torques this term will decide whether the box can carry the paper or a separate rig must. Given the September 2026 preprints, one of them on the same Go2, the window to publish the load-path explanation first is months, not years.
